## Stop Getting Tricked by Specs

A lot of people buy the wrong laptop. Not because they didn't do any research, but because they researched the wrong things. They spend hours comparing benchmark scores and processor names, then end up with a machine that doesn't actually fit what they bought it for.

So let's cut through the noise. If I'm looking at the performance of a laptop, these are the three specs I pay the most attention to, plus one extra thing that can completely change how those specs perform in the real world.

---

## 1. The CPU

<div className="max-w-100 mx-auto my-6">

![Task Manager showing the CPU](/blog/laptop-specs/cpu.jpg)

</div>

Intel and AMD are still the two big names here, but before comparing processor names, ask yourself one honest question: **what is this laptop actually going to do?**

If you're mostly gaming, don't automatically assume you need the most expensive CPU in the laptop. The GPU is usually going to have a bigger impact on how high you can push resolution and graphics settings. That doesn't mean the CPU doesn't matter, though. Competitive games, simulation games and anything targeting very high frame rates can still become heavily CPU-dependent.

For video editing, rendering, compiling large projects and other heavy workloads, CPU performance becomes much more important. Higher-power Intel HX and AMD Ryzen HX chips can make sense here, but don't buy purely based on the HX badge. A powerful processor inside a laptop with poor cooling can still perform worse than you expect.

Thin-and-light laptops are another example where chasing the highest-end CPU isn't always the best move. An efficient H or HS-class processor can give you more than enough performance while producing less heat and giving you better battery life.

The point isn't to pick the "best" processor. It's to match the processor to what you're actually going to do with the laptop.

---

## 2. The GPU

This is the one gamers, 3D artists, VFX artists and anyone working with GPU-accelerated software need to pay very close attention to.

The moment anyone mentions "graphics card," the conversation jumps straight to the highest-end RTX card available. Most people don't need that. **The best GPU is the one that fits your workload and your budget.**

For a budget gaming laptop in 2026, something around an RTX 5050 or RTX 5060 can already handle a lot of modern games very well. You might not be maxing every setting with ray tracing turned on, but you don't need a 5090 just to have a good gaming experience.

For video editing, Blender, 3D work, VFX or local AI workloads, I'd start paying much more attention to **VRAM**. A faster GPU is useful, but running out of VRAM in the middle of a large project can become a much bigger problem than losing a few frames in a game.

| Use Case                                  | What I'd Look For                                 |
| :---------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| Casual gaming / light creative work       | RTX 5050-class or equivalent                      |
| Gaming / video editing / content creation | RTX 5060–5070 class                               |
| Heavy 3D / VFX / AI workloads             | RTX 5070 Ti / 5080 and above, with plenty of VRAM |

<div className="max-w-100 mx-auto my-6">

![Task Manager showing the GPU](/blog/laptop-specs/gpu.jpg)

</div>

There is one more catch with laptop GPUs: **the name alone doesn't tell you the whole story.**

Two laptops can both say "RTX 5060" on the box and still perform differently because manufacturers can run the GPU at different power limits, and one laptop may have much better cooling than the other. This is why I wouldn't compare gaming laptops using the GPU name alone.

Check actual reviews of the specific laptop you're buying, especially its GPU power, temperatures and gaming performance.

Don't buy more GPU than you need. But definitely don't buy less either.

---

## 3. RAM

And this is the one I'd really push you not to cheap out on.

You can have a great CPU and GPU and still make the entire laptop feel worse by not giving it enough memory. Once you're constantly running out of RAM, Windows starts relying more heavily on storage, apps reload more often and multitasking becomes annoying very quickly.

In 2026, **16GB is the minimum I'd recommend for most people buying a new laptop.**

<Callout>
  **8GB can still be enough** if your use is genuinely basic: browsing,
  schoolwork, Office apps, streaming and other light tasks. But if you're
  gaming, editing, programming with heavier tools or running several demanding
  apps at once, I'd start at 16GB.
</Callout>

<div className="max-w-100 mx-auto my-6">

![Task Manager showing the RAM](/blog/laptop-specs/ram.jpg)

</div>

For heavier creative work, 32GB is where I'd aim. Video editing, After Effects, large Photoshop files, development tools, virtual machines and 3D software can eat through 16GB surprisingly quickly when you're multitasking.

Also check whether the RAM is actually upgradeable. Some laptops have replaceable SODIMMs, some have part of the memory soldered, and others solder everything permanently to the motherboard. If the laptop can't be upgraded later, buying enough RAM from the beginning matters a lot more.

---

<h2 className="text-white!">The Bonus One: Cooling</h2>

This doesn't normally get a big number on the product page, but it can decide how well everything above actually performs.

A laptop with a powerful CPU and GPU still needs to get rid of the heat they produce. If the cooling system can't keep up, the laptop reduces power and clock speeds to keep temperatures under control. That's thermal throttling, and it's one of the reasons two laptops with similar specifications can perform very differently.

<div className="max-w-100 mx-auto my-6">

![Laptop underside showing the cooling vents](/blog/laptop-specs/cooling.jpg)

</div>

This is especially important with gaming laptops and thin performance machines. Don't just look at the CPU and GPU names on the product page. Find reviews of the exact laptop and check its temperatures, sustained performance, fan noise and GPU power limits.

A slightly slower chip inside a well-cooled laptop can be a much better buy than a higher-end chip stuffed into a chassis that can't properly handle it.
